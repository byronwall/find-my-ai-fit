# 05 — Take-home library by part of your business

Ready-to-run prompts for the workloads where small and mid-market businesses see value first. Every prompt passes the BLAH filter.

## Every business — start here

The cross-cutting workhorses. Whatever you sell, you do these every week.

### Meeting notes → summary & actions

The workhorse. Paste any transcript or messy notes.

```text
Here are my meeting notes: [paste notes or transcript]. Give me three things: 1) a 5-bullet summary, 2) an action-item table (owner, task, deadline), 3) a short follow-up email to attendees confirming decisions and next steps. Flag anything that was discussed but left unresolved.
```

### Email drafter & tone-shifter

Bullet points in, finished email out — then shift the tone until it sounds like you.

```text
Draft an email from me to [recipient and their relationship to me — customer, vendor, my team]. What I need it to say: [bullet points — messy is fine]. Tone: [friendly / direct / apologetic / firm]. Keep it under 150 words with a clear ask at the end.

Follow-ups as needed:
Make it warmer. / Make it firmer. / Make it shorter. / Sound less corporate.
```

### Long document summarizer

Contracts, reports, RFPs — anything you've been avoiding reading.

```text
Here is a document: [paste or attach it]. Give me: 1) a plain-language summary in 5 bullets, 2) every date, deadline, and dollar amount in a list, 3) anything unusual, risky, or worth a closer look, 4) the three questions I should ask before signing or acting on this. Do not skip the fine print.
```

### Plain-language translator

Turn jargon — legal, technical, insurance, government — into words a normal person can act on.

```text
Here is a piece of [contract language / technical documentation / insurance policy / government regulation]: [paste it]. Rewrite it in plain language at an 8th-grade reading level. Then tell me: 1) what it actually requires me to do, 2) any deadlines or costs hiding in it, 3) anything a careful person would push back on.
```

### SOP from a brain dump

Get the process out of your head (or your best employee's) and into a document.

```text
I'm going to brain-dump how we do [process] at my business. Turn it into a clean step-by-step SOP a brand-new employee could follow: numbered steps, who's responsible, common mistakes to avoid, and what "done" looks like. Ask me clarifying questions about any step that's vague. Here's the dump: [talk or type it all out — messy is fine]
```

### The difficult customer reply

De-escalate without over-promising — with your judgment as the final gate.

```text
A customer sent this complaint: [paste it]. Context: [what actually happened, and what you're willing to offer]. Draft a reply that acknowledges the frustration specifically, explains without excuses, and offers [your remedy]. Warm, direct, under 150 words. Then give me a second version that's slightly firmer, so I can choose.
```

### Vendor quote comparison

Three quotes, one decision — without re-reading them five times.

```text
I have [number] quotes for [what you're buying]. Here they are: [paste them]. Build a comparison table: total cost, what's included, what's excluded, timeline, warranty/support terms, and red flags. Note where the quotes aren't comparing apples to apples, list the clarifying question I should ask each vendor, and tell me which one you'd lean toward and why.
```

## Sales & business development

### Proposal & quote drafting

Turn a call or scope notes into a client-ready first draft.

```text
You're a proposal writer for [my business — one sentence]. Here are my notes from the client conversation: [paste notes]. Draft a proposal with: a 2-sentence summary of their need, our recommended approach, scope (what's included AND what's not), timeline, and pricing section using these numbers: [pricing]. Professional but human tone. Ask me up to 3 questions first if anything is unclear.
```

### Quote follow-up that reframes value

After a quote goes quiet or gets pushback, move the conversation from your price to the cost of doing nothing.

```text
I sent a quote for [product/service] to [prospect — who they are and what they do], and the response was [paste it — e.g., "too expensive," "what we have works fine," "need to think about it"]. Write me a follow-up that reframes the real cost of their current approach — [the time, risk, or money their status quo is costing them] — and makes the value concrete with their numbers where I've given them. Short, confident, no pressure tactics, one clear next step.
```

### Cold outreach that doesn't sound cold

A first-touch email built on relevance, not flattery.

```text
I want to reach out to [prospect — role and company type] about [what you offer]. What I know about them: [anything real — their business, a trigger event, a mutual connection, something they published]. Write a first-touch email under 100 words: open with something specific to THEM (not praise), one sentence on the problem we solve, one proof point, and a low-commitment ask ([e.g., "worth a 15-minute call?"]). No "I hope this finds you well," no exclamation points, no jargon.
```

### Competitive & market research brief

Use a tool with web access for current results.

```text
Research [competitor or market] for me. I run [my business — one sentence]. Build a brief covering: what they offer and at what price points, how they position themselves, what customers praise and complain about in reviews, and where they're weak. End with the three most actionable opportunities for a business like mine. Cite your sources so I can verify.
```

### Overdue invoice sequence

Three escalating emails, written once, used forever.

```text
Write a 3-email sequence for overdue invoices at [my business]: Email 1 (7 days overdue) — friendly nudge, assume oversight. Email 2 (30 days) — direct, restate amount and due date, offer to discuss. Email 3 (60 days) — firm but professional, state next steps. Placeholders for [name], [invoice #], [amount]. Keep each under 100 words. I want to preserve the relationship in all three.
```

## Marketing & customer growth

### A month of marketing in one sitting

Content calendar plus first drafts, from one prompt.

```text
My business: [one sentence — who you serve and what you sell]. Create a 4-week content calendar for [channels — e.g., email + Facebook + LinkedIn]: 2 posts per week, mixing helpful tips, behind-the-scenes, and one promotion. Present as a table: date, channel, topic, first-draft copy. My voice: [plainspoken / fun / expert]. No hype words, no exclamation points.
```

### Review responses — the good and the ugly

Answer every review like an owner who cares, in two minutes.

```text
Here is a customer review of my business: [paste it]. Context if needed: [what actually happened]. Draft a response as the owner: thank them specifically (no generic "thanks for your feedback"), address anything they raised honestly, and — if the review is negative — take the conversation offline with a direct way to reach me. Under 100 words, no defensiveness, no corporate apology language.
```

### Service page & local listing copy

The words that make your Google listing and website actually say what you do.

```text
My business: [what you do, who for, and your service area]. What makes us different: [1–3 honest things — speed, guarantee, experience, specialty]. Write: 1) a Google Business profile description (max 750 characters), 2) a homepage headline + subheadline, 3) a short "About us" paragraph that sounds like a person wrote it. Plain language a customer would actually search for — no "premier," no "solutions," no "passion for excellence."
```

### Turn your expertise into content

You already know things your customers would love to read. Extract them.

```text
I'm an expert in [your field]. Interview me for 5 questions about the mistakes customers make, the questions I answer constantly, and what people misunderstand about [topic]. Then turn my answers into: 1) a short blog post, 2) three social posts, 3) an email to my list — all in my voice, no jargon, no hype.
```

## People & operations

### Job posting + interview kit

Posting, screening questions, and interview guide in one pass.

```text
I'm hiring a [role] for [my business — one sentence]. Key responsibilities: [list]. Must-haves: [list]. Pay range: [range]. Create: 1) a job posting that sounds like a real person wrote it, 2) five phone-screen questions with what a good answer sounds like, 3) three practical interview exercises that test the actual work.
```

### 30-day onboarding plan

So your new hire's first month isn't "shadow whoever's least busy."

```text
I just hired a [role] at [my business — one sentence]. Their responsibilities: [list]. Build a 30-day onboarding plan: week-by-week goals, what they should be able to do on their own by each Friday, who they should spend time with and why, and three check-in questions I should ask at days 7, 14, and 30. Keep it realistic for a small business — no corporate onboarding theater.
```

### The hard conversation prep

Performance issues, missed commitments, attitude problems — walk in prepared, not scripted.

```text
I need to have a difficult conversation with [an employee / a partner / a vendor] about [the issue — be specific about behavior, not personality]. What I want to happen afterward: [the outcome]. Help me prepare: 1) how to open the conversation in two sentences without softening it into mush, 2) the specific examples I should have ready, 3) how they're likely to respond and how I handle each, 4) what a fair next step and timeline looks like. Then play the other person so I can practice.
```

### Policy & handbook drafting

First drafts of the documents you've been putting off. For an AI policy specifically, the site recommends PolicyPro™ in the BIG AI Studio.

```text
Draft a [PTO / remote work / expense / AI use] policy for a [size]-person [type] business in Indiana. Plain language a normal person can read, one page max. Cover: what's allowed, what's not, how to ask for exceptions, and who decides. Flag with [REVIEW] anything I should confirm with an attorney or accountant rather than take your word for.
```

## Leadership

### The weekly team update

Five bullet points in, an update your team will actually read.

```text
Turn these notes into my weekly team update: [bullets — wins, changes, priorities, anything people need to know]. Format: 2-sentence opener that sets the tone, then "What happened," "What's coming," and "Where I need help." Sound like me talking, not a newsletter — [plainspoken / direct / upbeat]. Under 250 words.
```

### The decision one-pager

Force a fuzzy decision into a page you can act on — or hand to a partner.

```text
I'm deciding whether to [the decision]. Context: [key facts, numbers, constraints, deadline]. Build me a one-page decision brief: the decision in one sentence, the options (including "do nothing"), what each costs and risks, what would have to be true for each to be the right call, and your recommendation with reasoning. End with the one piece of missing information that would most change the answer.
```

### Your Monday plan (the 90-day commitment)

You wrote down three commitments in the room. This prompt turns them into a plan.

```text
At a workshop today I committed to three things: one AI use case I'll sponsor ([your use case]), one behavior I'll model ([your behavior]), and one expectation I'll set for my team ([your expectation]). Build me a simple 90-day plan: what to do in week 1, month 1, and by day 90. Keep it realistic for a busy owner — 30 minutes a week of effort, not a transformation program. End with the exact words I could say to my team this week.
```
